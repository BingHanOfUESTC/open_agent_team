# Speaker Notes — Vincent van Gogh: A Life in Color

## Slide 1: Vincent van Gogh: A Life in Color
Welcome. Today we're going to walk through the life of Vincent van Gogh — not just the iconic paintings, but the story of how they came to be. In just ten years, he created over 2,100 artworks. He sold only one painting in his lifetime. And yet today, his Starry Night is one of the most recognized images on earth. How did that happen? That's the journey we're taking.

## Slide 2: He Sold One Painting in His Lifetime. Today, His Work Defines Modern Art.
Let me start with a paradox. Vincent van Gogh is one of the most famous artists in history — but during his lifetime, he was almost completely unknown. He sold exactly one painting while he was alive, for the equivalent of about 80 dollars today. A century later, one of his portraits sold for 82.5 million dollars. The question is: what happened in between? And the answer is a story of relentless creation against every imaginable obstacle.

## Slide 3: He Tried Being an Art Dealer, a Teacher, and a Preacher. None of It Worked.
Van Gogh didn't grow up dreaming of being an artist. He tried being an art dealer, a teacher in England, and a missionary to coal miners in Belgium. He was fired from the church for being too zealous — for actually living among the poor instead of just preaching at them. By age 27, he had failed at everything he'd tried. And that's exactly when he finally found his calling.

## Slide 4: In 1880, at Age 27, He Found His True Calling in the Depths of Crisis
After the church turned him away, Van Gogh wrote these words to his brother Theo. He wasn't abandoning his mission to serve the poor — he was translating it into a new language: paint. When he signed his paintings 'Vincent,' he said, it was as one of them — one of the working people, the suffering people. This was the seed of everything that followed. He was 27, with no formal training, and yet this was the moment he became an artist.

## Slide 5: The Dutch Years
For the next six years, Van Gogh taught himself to be an artist in the Netherlands. These are the years of dark earth tones, peasant subjects, and the slow, painful acquisition of craft. He worked in Etten, The Hague, Drenthe, Nuenen, and briefly Antwerp — each place adding a layer to his understanding. We begin with his first masterpiece.

## Slide 6: "They Have Tilled the Earth Themselves with the Same Hands They Are Putting in the Dish."
This is The Potato Eaters — Van Gogh's first major work. Look at the hands. These are hands that have worked the soil all day, and now they're reaching for the same potatoes they planted. Van Gogh said he wanted the painting to smell of bacon, smoke, and potato steam. It's dark, it's coarse, and it's completely intentional. This is not a pretty picture of rural life — it's an honest one. He painted over forty studies of peasant heads to prepare for this single composition.

## Slide 7: In Two Years, Paris Transformed Everything He Knew About Color.
When Van Gogh arrived in Paris in 1886, he was still painting in dark, muddy tones. Two years later, he left for the south of France with a palette exploding in color. What happened? He met the Impressionists. He saw what Seurat was doing with dots of pure color. He fell in love with Japanese prints. And he painted himself over and over — more than 20 self-portraits — using his own face as a laboratory for color. Remarkably, this bright self-portrait was painted on the back of an old dark Dutch peasant study. The transformation was literally two sides of the same canvas.

## Slide 8: Two Years Changed Everything: From the Dark North to the Color of Light.
Look at these two paintings, made barely two years apart. On the left, The Weaver from his time in Nuenen — dark, heavy, the brown of the Dutch interior. On the right, The Courtesan from Paris — a direct copy of a Japanese woodblock print, alive with vibrant reds, yellows, and decorative patterns. This is not just a change in taste. This is a complete reinvention of how Van Gogh saw the world. Paris gave him new eyes — and Japanese art gave him a new visual vocabulary.

## Slide 9: Arles — The Southern Light
In February 1888, Van Gogh left Paris and headed south — to Arles, in Provence. He was looking for something: a clarity of light, a community of artists, a place where color could burn as bright as the Mediterranean sun. What followed were the most productive 15 months of his life — and the most shattering crisis he had ever faced. The Yellow House, the Sunflowers, the night cafés — this is where Van Gogh became Van Gogh.

## Slide 10: He Painted Sunflowers to Decorate a Room for a Friend Who Would Soon Betray Him.
Van Gogh rented this small yellow house in Arles and began preparing for the arrival of Paul Gauguin, an artist he deeply admired. He painted the Sunflowers — not as a casual still life, but as a gift, a welcome, an offering to a colleague he hoped would become his collaborator in a new artists' community. This was a moment of pure optimism. He believed he was building something — a studio, a community, a home for a new kind of art. He didn't know that in just a few months, it would all come crashing down.

## Slide 11: He Didn't Paint the Night Black. He Painted It in Blues, Greens, and Gold.
Look at this painting — Café Terrace at Night. There is no black in this night sky. Van Gogh painted the darkness using blues, violets, and greens. He painted the café light in gold. He understood something revolutionary: that night isn't the absence of light — it's a different quality of light entirely. The stars aren't just pinpricks — they're living presences. He painted this on the street, using gas lamps to see his canvas. Think about that — an artist standing on a cobblestone street in 1888, revolutionizing how we see the night.

## Slide 12: Gauguin Arrived on October 23. By December 23, Van Gogh Had Cut Off His Own Ear.
This is the story that everyone knows — or thinks they know. Gauguin came to Arles in October 1888. For nine weeks, they painted together, drank together, argued furiously. Then on December 23, Van Gogh cut off part of his ear. But here's what's often missed: after being released from the hospital in January 1889, the first thing he did was paint. Even in the aftermath of catastrophe, he kept working. The Studio of the South was dead. But the painting never stopped.

## Slide 13: Saint-Rémy — Painting as Survival
In May 1889, fearing another breakdown, Van Gogh voluntarily checked himself into the Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum in Saint-Rémy. He would spend a year there. Confined to the asylum grounds, then a walled garden, and eventually allowed brief excursions into the countryside — he painted about 150 canvases. Including the single most famous painting in the history of art. This is the paradox at the heart of Van Gogh's story: in the place of his greatest confinement, he produced his most transcendent work.

## Slide 14: He Painted the Entire Cosmos from a Room in an Asylum.
This is it. The Starry Night — painted in June 1889, from a window in a mental asylum. That cypress tree in the foreground? It wasn't actually there. Van Gogh added it from memory. The village below? Idealized. The sky — those swirling, pulsing currents of blue and gold — that's not what you see when you look up. That's what you feel. This is the work of an artist who had lost almost everything, but who could still see, and paint, the sublime. Let's just look at it for a moment.

## Slide 15: In the Garden of an Asylum, He Found the Entire Natural World.
Take a moment to scan these three works, all created during Van Gogh's year in the asylum at Saint-Rémy. Irises — painted in the garden in his first weeks, full of life and vibrant color. Wheat Field with Cypresses — that dark, flame-like tree he compared to an Egyptian obelisk, the Provençal landscape transformed into something almost visionary. And his Self-Portrait with Bandaged Ear — an unflinching look at himself after the crisis, a document of survival and self-knowledge. In the span of this one year, confined and suffering, he produced some of the most life-affirming art the world has ever seen. This was not a man giving up — this was a man holding on through the only means he had.

## Slide 16: He Averaged a Painting a Day. He Had 70 Days Left.
Van Gogh left the asylum in May 1890 and moved to Auvers-sur-Oise, a small town just north of Paris. He was 37 years old. In the 70 days he had left, he painted more than 70 canvases — a painting a day. This is the portrait of Dr. Gachet, the physician tasked with his care. Look at the expression on his face — the weariness, the sadness, the foxglove plant on the table — digitalis, a heart medicine. Van Gogh later wrote to Theo: 'He is sicker than I am, I think.' It was to be Van Gogh's final productive period, a desperate, beautiful creative burst.

## Slide 17: Two Weeks Before the End: "I Feel Like a Failure. I Am in a Mood of Almost Too Much Calm."
In the last month of his life, Van Gogh's letters tell a story of violent emotional swings. On July 10th, 1890, he writes to Theo that he feels like a failure and sees no happy future. Just two days later, he tells his mother he is in a mood of 'almost too much calm.' And on July 23rd — in what may have been his last letter to Theo — he says he is giving his canvases his undivided attention and is trying to do as well as the painters he most admired. This is not a simple story of despair. It's a story of someone holding on to painting as the one thing that still made sense.

## Slide 18: He Walked Into a Wheat Field on July 27, 1890. Two Days Later, He Was Gone.
On July 27, 1890, Van Gogh walked into a wheat field and shot himself. He didn't die instantly — he walked back to the inn, climbed the stairs to his room, and lay on his bed. Theo arrived the next day. They talked through the night. Vincent's last words to his brother were: 'The sadness will last forever.' He died at 1:30 in the morning on July 29th. At his funeral, his coffin was surrounded by his last canvases and masses of yellow flowers — dahlias and sunflowers. Theo died six months later. They are buried side by side in the cemetery at Auvers, two brothers reunited in the wheat fields.

## Slide 19: In One Decade, He Reinvented What a Painting Could Be.
Here's the arc of Van Gogh's entire creative life — six periods, ten years. From the dark, heavy brushstrokes of the Dutch period in The Hague and Nuenen, through the color explosion of Paris and the full maturation in Arles, to the transcendent, emotional landscapes of Saint-Rémy and the desperate final burst at Auvers. Notice: he never stayed still. Each period was an experiment, a reaching for something he hadn't done before. He taught himself everything — and in the process, he taught the world a new way to see. The progress is staggering when you see it all laid out like this.

## Slide 20: "The Sadness Will Last Forever." But So Does the Art.
Van Gogh's last words were 'The sadness will last forever.' And in one sense, the sadness of his story does endure — the struggle, the loneliness, the early death at 37. But here's what also endures: 2,100 artworks. 820 letters. A body of work that changed the course of modern art. His paintings hang in every major museum in the world. His letters have been read by millions. Van Gogh spent his whole life trying to communicate something — first as a preacher, then as an artist. He failed as a preacher. But as a painter, he found his congregation. And his voice has never stopped speaking. Thank you.
